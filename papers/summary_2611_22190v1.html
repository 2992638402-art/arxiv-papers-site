Emergence and Collapse of Leader-Follower Flocks through Phantom Targets: Surprise Nullification in Bayesian and Inverse Bayesian Inference

arXiv ID: 2611.22190
分类: cs.MA, nlin.AO
发布时间: 2026-11-17

📄 论文信息

  • 作者: Shuji Shinohara, Daiki Morita, Yoshihiro Nakajima, Takeshi Takano, Masakazu Higuchi, Ung-il Chung, Yukio-Pegio Gunji
  • PDF: 下载

📝 摘要

In Bayesian and inverse Bayesian (BIB) inference, a large prediction error can trigger surprise nullification, which expands belief and likelihood variances by a common factor, leaving the updated variance ratio the same as in standard Bayesian updating. This can increase belief uncertainty while keeping the learning rate low. In the simulations, three agents used BIB inference to predict one another's positions and moved toward those predictions, repeatedly forming leader-follower flocks that subsequently collapsed despite having no alignment rules or designated leaders. Three leadership criteria (front position, heading stability, and flock initiation) identified the same agent in 89% of eligible flocks, with different agents serving as leaders across flocks. Flocks formed when an agent nearly stopped incorporating another's observations (link cut) and moved straight toward a predicted position detached from reality (phantom target), thereby attracting followers. Link cuts arose when two successive resets assigned a very small and then an ordinary likelihood variance, sharply increasing the likelihood-to-belief variance ratio. Flocks collapsed upon link recovery or when returning phantom targets balanced the leader's forward and backward attractions. Without nullification, cuts persisted throughout the analysis period; expanding only belief variance caused recovery within a few steps. Expanding likelihood variance as well allowed cuts to persist while belief uncertainty grew, enabling later resets and recovery. Thus, nullification preserves relative precision, allowing cuts and flocks to persist, while reducing absolute precision to enable recovery and collapse, reconciling these seemingly opposing processes through a single operation. These results suggest that, in this model, individually inaccurate predictions can give rise to collective order.
